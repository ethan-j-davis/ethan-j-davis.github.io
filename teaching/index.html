Teaching Philosophy

When I teach mathematics, the sentence I hear most often from students is some version of “I’ve never seen this problem before, so I don’t know how to do it.” Behind it is a belief many students bring from years of math classes: that mathematics is a catalog of procedures to memorize, and that a problem which doesn’t exactly match a worked example is out of reach. Much of my teaching is aimed at undoing that belief. I want students to leave my courses seeing mathematics as a way of thinking that they can use, not a list of steps they were handed.

Context comes first. Too often, introductory mathematics is presented as a prerequisite to get through: definitions, then formulas, then exercises, with little explanation of where the ideas came from or why anyone cared. Students reasonably conclude that what they are learning has no value beyond the next exam. I try to put context at the front of every topic. That means the question that motivated an idea before its formal definition, the intuition behind a technique before the computation, the connections between topics, and the places the mathematics shows up in students’ own fields.

  • In Applied Calculus, that might mean starting with a business question about the cost of producing one more unit and letting the derivative appear as the natural answer.
  • In Discrete Mathematics for information technology students, it can mean treating logic and proof as the same reasoning they use when debugging a program. Beyond any single application, I want students to recognize what mathematics trains: thinking carefully, making deductions, and combining separate ideas into a solution. Those skills matter in every discipline.

Try something. When students get stuck, the question they usually ask is “What should I do here?”, as if every problem has one correct first move and their job is to guess it. I try to replace that with a different question: “What can I do?” Instead of showing the solution, I ask, “What do you know?” or “Does anything here look familiar?” I encourage students to write down the given information, a definition that seems related, or even just what they find interesting or confusing about the problem. “Should” asks students to know the answer before they begin. “Can” invites them to explore, and almost always there is something they can do. Building confidence, and countering the learned helplessness that so many students carry, is one of the most important things I can do in a classroom.

Fluency makes understanding possible. Mathematics is a skill, and like any skill it takes practice. I value regular, low-stakes assessments such as weekly quizzes because they build fluency with the fundamentals, like the language of mathematics and the basic mechanics of differentiation and integration, so that students have something solid to reason with. I see fluency and conceptual understanding not as competing goals but as partners: practice gives students the vocabulary, and context gives them a reason to use it.

Teaching for the room in front of me. I have taught as instructor of record at the University of Cincinnati since 2024, in courses ranging from College Algebra to Multivariable Calculus, in person, in a flipped classroom, in compressed summer sessions, and fully online. Many of these courses had set content and assigned problems, but I found real freedom in how I communicated the concepts behind them. In my flipped courses, many students had not watched the pre-lecture videos, so I learned to explain each idea in a way that reached everyone in the room, whether it was their first time seeing it or their third. That experience taught me to meet my students where they actually are, rather than where a syllabus assumes they will be.

Looking ahead. I am eager to teach courses where I can design the structure around these ideas: motivating each unit with a guiding question, building in regular practice, and creating assessments that reward reasoning alongside computation. Above all, I want every student who leaves my class to understand not only how to solve a problem, but why it was worth solving.

Courses

2026

MATH 1071: Introduction to Discrete Mathematics

Spring

Logic, sets, methods of proof, induction, functions, counting and probability, and graph theory, for students in information technology and programming.

2025

MATH 1045: Applied Calculus II

Spring and Fall

Second semester of the applied calculus sequence: integration and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, applications to economics and probability, and calculus of two variables. Taught Spring 2025 and Fall 2025.

MATH 2063: Multivariable Calculus

Summer

Lines and planes in space, vector-valued functions, partial derivatives and their applications, multiple integrals, and vector calculus, taught as a 13-week asynchronous online course.

2024

MATH 1044: Applied Calculus I

Fall

First semester of a two-course calculus sequence for students in business and the life sciences: functions, limits, derivatives, and their applications to optimization and marginal analysis.

MATH 1021: College Algebra

Summer

Linear, quadratic, polynomial, rational, exponential, and logarithmic functions, systems of linear equations, and modeling with functions, taught in a compressed summer session.
